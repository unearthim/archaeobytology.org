Module 9: Public Scholarship Project

Duration: 1 week Type: Public Engagement & Translation

Module Overview

Academic knowledge means nothing if it stays in the academy.

In this module, you'll translate your archaeobytological research into accessible public scholarship. You'll write for general audiences, create engaging media, and practice the art of making complex ideas compelling.

Learning Objectives

1. Write for public audiences: Translate technical concepts without dumbing down 2. Choose appropriate formats: Match medium to message and audience 3. Engage beyond academia: Reach people who will never read a textbook 4. Build public voice: Develop your perspective as a public intellectual 5. Create impact: Make scholarship that changes how people think and act

Required Readings

- Textbook Chapter 17: The Public Intellectual in Archaeobytology - Examples: Cory Doctorow's blog, Molly White's "Web3 is Going Just Great," Jason Scott's talks

Assignment: Create Public-Facing Scholarship

Choose ONE format and create polished public-facing work:

Option A: Long-Form Essay (2,000-3,000 words)

Write for a general-interest publication (The Atlantic, WIRED, The Verge, Jacobin, etc.)

Requirements: - Engaging opening that hooks non-expert readers - Clear explanation of archaeobytological concepts - Compelling narrative or argument - Real-world examples and stories - Call to action or implications for readers - No jargon (or jargon clearly explained)

Possible Topics: - "What We Lose When Platforms Die" - "The Case for Digital Sovereignty" - "Inside the Mission to Save the Internet" - "Why Your Data Should Outlive the Companies That Hold It"

Option B: Video Essay (8-12 minutes)

Create an explanatory video for YouTube, TikTok, or similar platform.

Requirements: - Visually engaging (graphics, b-roll, demonstrations) - Clear script with logical structure - Accessible to non-expert viewers - Production quality (good audio, editing, pacing) - Includes sources/references

Possible Topics: - Autopsy of a platform death (GeoCities, Google Reader, etc.) - "How to Make Your Digital Life Un-Murderable" - "What is Digital Sovereignty and Why Should You Care?" - "The Three Pillars Explained"

Option C: Interactive Explainer

Build a web-based interactive experience.

Requirements: - Guides users through archaeobytological concepts - Interactive elements (quizzes, simulators, choose-your-own-adventure) - Works on mobile and desktop - Clear navigation and progression - Visual design supports learning

Possible Topics: - "Audit Your Digital Sovereignty" (interactive checklist) - "Triage Simulator" (simplified version of Module 4) - "Platform Autopsy Kit" (guide users through analyzing a dead platform) - "Build Your Own Sovereign System" (decision tree/guide)

Option D: Podcast Episode (20-30 minutes)

Produce a podcast episode explaining archaeobytological concepts.

Requirements: - Professional audio quality - Engaging narrative structure - Interviews or multiple voices (if applicable) - Clear explanations without talking down to audience - Show notes with references and links

Possible Topics: - Interview with digital preservationist - Deep dive on a platform murder - "How To" guide (preserve your data, choose sovereign tools) - Debate/discussion on digital preservation ethics

Option E: Social Media Campaign

Create a week-long educational campaign on Twitter, Instagram, Mastodon, or TikTok.

Requirements: - 7 days of daily content (at least one post per day) - Mix of formats (text, images, video, threads) - Cohesive narrative arc across the week - Engaging and shareable - Actually post it (don't just plan it)

Possible Topics: - "7 Days of Platform Deaths" (one autopsy per day) - "Build Digital Sovereignty in 7 Steps" - "The Archive & Anvil Explained" (series) - "Digital Preservation Myths Debunked"

Assignment Deliverables

1. Public Scholarship Piece (your chosen format)

Polished, publication-ready work.

2. Audience Analysis (2-3 pages)

Write a reflection addressing: - Who is your target audience? - What do they already know/care about? - What do you want them to understand or do after engaging with your work? - What choices did you make to reach them effectively? - What compromises or simplifications did you make?

3. Distribution Plan (1-2 pages)

How will you get this in front of your target audience? - Where will you publish/post? - How will you promote it? - What communities or influencers might share it? - What's your call to action?

4. Impact Assessment (after posting)

After your work is public for at least 3 days: - What engagement did it get? (views, shares, comments) - What feedback did you receive? - Did you reach your target audience? - What would you do differently next time?

Assessment Rubric

Quality of Public Scholarship (50 points)

- Clarity (20 pts): Complex ideas explained accessibly - Engagement (15 pts): Compelling and interesting, not boring - Accuracy (10 pts): Faithful to archaeobytological principles - Production quality (5 pts): Professional presentation

Audience Understanding (20 points)

- Analysis (10 pts): Clear understanding of target audience - Strategic choices (10 pts): Format and framing match audience needs

Distribution & Impact (20 points)

- Distribution plan (10 pts): Realistic strategy for reaching audience - Actual reach (10 pts): Evidence of posting and engagement

Reflection (10 points)

- Self-awareness (5 pts): Recognizes strengths and weaknesses - Learning (5 pts): Articulates lessons for future public scholarship

Examples of Excellent Public Scholarship

Essays: - Anil Dash, "The Web We Lost" - Maciej Cegłowski, "The Website Obesity Crisis" - Audrey Watters, various posts on ed-tech

Videos: - Folding Ideas (Dan Olson) - "The Problem With NFTs" - ContraPoints - Complex ideas with high production value - Vox Explainers - Visual storytelling

Interactive: - Neal Agarwal's "The Size of Space" - Nicky Case's "We Become What We Behold" - ProPublica's interactive investigations

Podcasts: - Reply All (before implosion) - This Machine Kills (tech criticism) - Tech Won't Save Us
